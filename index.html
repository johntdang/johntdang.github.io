Control Arm Design

2023 · UCR Highlander Racing — Suspension Associate Engineer

A double wishbone control arm in 4130 steel normalized tubing, engineered for a 5,000 N critical buckling load — the part that locates the wheel and carries cornering and braking loads from the tire contact patch into the chassis.

3.0 FOSUnder 3.5 kN longitudinal & 2.8 kN lateral loads (MATLAB + hand calcs)
9.75 mm IDUniversal bore; 10.636–10.91 mm OD scaled by arm length
5,000 NCritical buckling load the tubing was engineered around
4130 steelNormalized tubing, welded to a machined bearing housing
SolidWorks4130 SteelMATLABGD&T

Overview

On a Formula SAE car, the control arms are the structural link between the wheels and the chassis. Every load the tire generates — braking, cornering, bumps — passes through them, and because they work as slender tubes in compression, buckling is the failure mode that drives the design. This arm was designed for UCR Highlander Racing's double wishbone suspension, where the geometry also has to hold the wheel's alignment precisely as the suspension moves.

Design approach

The arm is built from 4130 steel normalized tubing, welded to a custom-machined steel housing containing Aurora HAB4T bearings. Grade 8 fasteners and Aurora rod ends handle the chassis mounting, so the arm can be installed, adjusted, and serviced with standard hardware. A single universal bore of 9.75 mm ID runs across the arm family, with the tube OD scaled by arm length — 10.636 to 10.91 mm — so longer, more heavily loaded arms get more material without redesigning the interfaces.

Validation & manufacturing

The design was engineered to maintain a strict 2.5 factor of safety in the original buckling analysis, and the final configuration reaches a 3.0 factor of safety under 3.5 kN longitudinal and 2.8 kN lateral loads, sized with MATLAB and hand calculations. As Suspension Associate Engineer, John also released the 2D drawings with strict GD&T, so the part that came off the welder matched the part that was analyzed.

Front Corner Assembly

2025 · UCR Highlander Racing

A complete front suspension corner modeled in SolidWorks and mirrored to build the full front axle — the master model the team used for FEA, kinematics, and manufacturing.

0.42° / 1 mmCamber change verified per 1 mm shim
Full axleSymmetrical load distribution & alignment in master CAD
SolidWorksFEAKinematics

Overview

A single corner — upper and lower control arms, pushrod linkages, and outboard pickup points — only tells half the story. Loads, alignment, and steering behavior emerge at the axle level, so this project took the single-corner design and mirrored it into a complete front track model in one master CAD assembly.

Design approach

The mirrored assembly duplicated the exact geometry of the upper and lower control arms, pushrod linkages, and outboard pickup points from the single-corner design, keeping both sides perfectly consistent. Working in a single master model meant a change on one side propagated correctly to the other, and the symmetrical layout guaranteed even load distribution and alignment across the axle.

Validation

The complete front track model enabled accurate finite element analysis and kinematics simulations that a single corner couldn't support. Camber behavior was verified directly from the model: 0.42° of camber change per 1 mm shim, giving the team a predictable, repeatable way to tune alignment at the track. Having the full axle validated digitally also made the transition from digital prototype to manufacturing the physical parts much smoother.

Pushrod Mounts — Front & Rear

2025 · UCR Highlander Racing

Sheet-metal mounts that interface the front and rear pushrods to the outboard pickup points in a double-shear configuration, as competition rules require — one shared job, solved two different ways for the front and rear packaging.

Front: 49.9 g + 15.5 gBase plate + each tab, 1/16″ sheet metal
Front: $18 / unitTotal manufacturing cost — front mount
Rear: 23.7 g + 7.39 gFolded base + auxiliary tab, 1/16″ sheet metal
Rear: $15 / unitReduced cost through fewer components
SolidWorksSheet MetalDouble ShearLightweighting

Overview

Pushrods carry the wheel loads from the control arm up to the rockers and dampers, and the mount where the pushrod meets the arm sees those loads under constant dynamic bending. Formula SAE rules require that joint to be captured in double shear, so each mount has to wrap the rod end on both sides — strong, precisely aligned, and light, because every gram outboard is unsprung mass. The front and rear mounts meet the same requirement, but their packaging led to two distinct designs.

Front — base plate & tabs

The front design uses a custom base plate with precision-cut slots that align and secure two individual tabs, creating the double-shear attachment. The base plate's edges are contoured to weld directly onto the tubular lower control arms, and the tabs are welded on both sides, so the load path from pushrod to arm is continuous steel with no bolted joints to loosen. The finished front mount weighs 49.9 g for the base plate plus 15.5 g per tab in 1/16″ sheet metal — about 81 g in total — and costs $18 per unit to manufacture.

Rear — folded single piece

The rear packaging left room to rethink how the part is made. Instead of a base plate plus two separate tabs, the rear design folds a single piece of sheet metal so the base intrinsically acts as one of the required structural tabs, eliminating redundant material and aligning for direct welding onto the tubular upper control arms. A single additional precision-cut tab slots into the base and is welded on both sides to complete the outboard pickup point and the double-shear capture. The folded base weighs 23.7 g and the auxiliary tab 7.39 g in 1/16″ sheet metal, and the unit cost drops to $15 through the reduced component count.

Results

Together, the pair shows the same structural idea adapted to its corner: the front prioritises a robust, slotted base-and-tabs assembly for optimal load transfer under dynamic bending, while the rear streamlines manufacturing — fewer parts to cut, fixture, and weld, and less unsprung mass at the wheel — while fully satisfying the structural and dynamic constraints. Both are priced and weighted for a student racing team to run on the car.

ALOHA Stationary Teleoperation

2025–26 · UCR Robotics & Explainable AI Lab (REAL) — Undergraduate Research Assistant

Bimanual teleoperation datasets and uncertainty-aware temporal action segmentation for toy-car assembly — bridging robot hardware and deep learning, and the work behind a co-authored paper on human-to-robot transfer learning.

73.45 edit scoreBest — human→robot transfer + gated corrector, mean over 3 seeds
55.23 → 72.51Robot-only → transfer (+17.28) with only 9 robot demonstrations
83 human · 9 robotUMI hand videos pretrain MS-TCN++; 18 ALOHA videos (9 demos) fine-tune it · 22 classes
0.755 AUROC · 95.38%Reliability analyzer AUROC; conformal coverage at the 93% target (sets avg. 17.41 / 22)
X3DMS-TCN++Transfer LearningConformal PredictionSolidWorksData Pipeline

Overview

Action segmentation supports intention estimation and execution monitoring in assisted teleoperation — the robot needs to recognize what the operator is doing, step by step, to assist reliably during long-horizon assembly. The problem is data: robot demonstrations are costly and hardware-limited to collect, while human demonstrations are far easier to gather and carry rich temporal structure. At the REAL Lab, John worked on both ends of that pipeline: operating the ALOHA stationary bimanual system to generate teleoperation demonstrations of toy-car assembly, and building the models that learn to recognize each step of the task as it happens.

Hardware & data

Dataset quality starts with the recording setup. John designed a custom 3D-modeled SolidWorks camera mount to stabilize recording, so the footage the models trained on stayed consistent across sessions. He then processed, annotated, and temporally segmented the footage — turning raw teleoperation video into labeled, multi-step action sequences across 22 toy-car assembly action classes, sharing a single 22-class vocabulary and 192-dimensional X3D-M features across the human and robot domains.

The paper’s cross-embodiment dataset pairs two sources. The human (source) domain is UMI hand demonstrations: 83 training videos plus 20 validation videos of toy-car assembly, captured with an egocentric GoPro on a handheld UMI gripper. The robot (target) domain is ALOHA teleoperation recorded with dual wrist cameras at 480×640, split into 18 training videos (9 demonstrations), 26 validation videos (13 demonstrations), 34 test videos (17 demonstrations), and 46 calibration videos. That imbalance is the point: the 83 human videos contain 4,025 observed action transitions, while the 18 robot training videos contain only 436 — too few on their own to learn the long-range ordering of a 22-step assembly, whose 22×22 transition matrix has 484 entries. Pretraining transfers those ordering priors to the robot.

The framework — three parts

The published framework combines three components. First, hierarchical transfer learning: MS-TCN++ (Multistage Temporal Convolutional Network) is pretrained on the human hand demonstrations and then fine-tuned on the limited robot teleoperation data, capturing both low-level actions and high-level task intentions with complete weight transfer and no architectural changes. Second, conformal prediction: temperature-scaled softmax probabilities are calibrated on a held-out split to produce frame-level prediction sets with statistical coverage guarantees — evaluated in both a standard (marginal) variant and a class-conditional variant with per-class thresholds. Third, reliability-gated segment correction: a reliability analyzer flags segments likely to be wrong, a gap-aware Transformer — also pretrained on human data and fine-tuned on robot data — proposes a corrected label, and a gate accepts the change only when the segment was flagged, the proposal confidence is at least 0.7, and the new label is transition-compatible with its neighbours. Together the system supports action recognition, temporal segmentation, intention anticipation, and mistake detection for assisted teleoperation.

Results — what the paper reports (v2)

All headline numbers below are from the published v2 paper (arXiv:2606.08341, revised 13 Oct 2026) and its project website, evaluated on the robot test set — 34 videos, 17 demonstrations — as a mean over 3 seeds. Training the model on robot data alone reaches a 55.23 edit score. Pretraining on human demonstrations and fine-tuning on just 9 robot demonstrations raises that to 72.51 — a gain of +17.28 — and lifts segmental F1@50 from 10.97 to 16.86 and frame accuracy from 28.48% to 34.11%. Adding the gated corrector gives the paper’s best result: a 73.45 edit score with 34.19% frame accuracy and F1@50 of 16.89, without lowering frame accuracy.

The uncertainty results are deliberately candid. The reliability analyzer identifies incorrect segments with 0.755 AUROC. Correction only helps when it is selective: applying corrections without the gate drops the edit score to 67.86, while gated acceptance holds 73.45 — mainly by safeguarding against harmful relabeling. Conformal prediction achieves 95.38% empirical coverage at its 93% target, but the prediction sets are large, averaging 17.41 of the 22 classes (class-conditional sets run about 17–27% smaller than standard conformal sets, which average about 21 of 22). The paper’s conclusion is balanced: human temporal pretraining clearly works, while reliable correction and informative uncertainty quantification remain hard with so little robot supervision.

About the paper

Uncertainty-Aware Action Segmentation for Human-to-Robot Assembly Teleoperation, arXiv:2606.08341 [cs.RO] — first submitted 6 June 2026, v2 revised 13 October 2026. Authors: Fnu Heman, Yixuan Wang, Kolin Xu, Conner Wallace, John Dang, Akhil Joshi, Vignesh Thondikulam, Jun Sheng, Pinhas Ben-Tzvi, and Mingyu Cai. John is a co-author through his REAL Lab work on the ALOHA data pipeline and segmentation models.

Capstone

BCI EEG Wheelchair

2025–26 · Senior Capstone, UC Riverside — Team Lead, 5 members

An EEG-based brain-computer interface wheelchair for independent mobility — driven by motor imagery, the user imagining movement, rather than following pre-set paths.

20 Hz · OpenBCIGanglion headset, FFT filtering, Python → Arduino Uno
Dual 350 W · 24 V 50 Ah43 A H-bridges, chain drive, 55-tooth sprocket at 3.0 FOS
OpenBCIPythonArduino / C++Team Lead — 5

Overview

For people who can't use a joystick, a wheelchair's controls are the barrier to independent mobility. This capstone set out to remove the hands from the loop entirely: read the user's intent from EEG signals and turn it into driving commands, with the user steering live rather than selecting from pre-programmed routes.

Design approach

Early on, a design matrix compared control paradigms and selected motor imagery over evoked potentials for live control. The signal chain runs from an OpenBCI Ganglion headset sampling at 20 Hz, through FFT filtering in Python, which sends directional commands over serial to an Arduino Uno driving two H-bridge controllers. On the mechanical side, dual 350 W motors on a 24 V 50 Ah supply drive the wheels through a chain drive with a 55-tooth sprocket sized at a 3.0 factor of safety, mounted with a custom PLA sprocket mount fastening the sprockets to the wheel hub. Ultrasonic sensors trigger autonomous deceleration for collision avoidance, so the chair protects the user even when a command is late or wrong.

Leading the team

John led the 5-member team from September 2025 to March 2026, managing the timeline with Gantt charts and validating decisions through weekly technical presentations and memo reports to faculty advisors — the same design, documentation, and review discipline the project needed to integrate EEG signal processing, embedded control, and a drivetrain into one working chair.

Airplane Bracket

2024 · Topology Optimization for Metal Additive Manufacturing

Topology optimization of a Titanium 6Al-4V aerospace bracket (B-0426-M), redesigned for metal additive manufacturing in Fusion 360 instead of being machined from solid stock.

10 kN ↑ · 5 kN →Pin loads; 4.5 kN bolt preload over 20 mm
1.5 FOS targetValidated with static stress simulation
Fusion 360Generative DesignFEA

Overview

A machined bracket carries the shape of its manufacturing process: material stays wherever the cutter couldn't reach, not where the loads need it. Moving the part to metal additive manufacturing removes that constraint — but only if the design is re-thought for the process. This project took the B-0426-M bracket through a full topology optimization workflow to find the shape the loads actually require.

Design approach

Preserve regions were defined at the mounting interfaces, so the optimizer couldn't touch the geometry that has to mate with the rest of the assembly, and obstacle geometries prevented any interference with surrounding parts. The bracket was loaded with 10 kN upward and 5 kN lateral pin loads, plus a 4.5 kN bolt preload distributed over 20 mm, against a 1.5 factor-of-safety target.

Validation

The final topology was evaluated by plotting mass against von Mises stress across design candidates — the trade curve that shows exactly what each gram saved costs in stress — followed by a complete static stress simulation to validate the chosen design before it would ever be printed.

Design Optimization

Robotic Arm Component

2024 · Shape Optimization & FEA

Shape optimization for a 50% mass reduction — removing material from low-stress areas and trimming stress concentrators, then proving with FEA that the lighter part is still comfortably strong.

85.541 MPaMax von Mises stress; 0.321 mm peak displacement
5.88 min FOSWell above the 1.5 minimum requirement
Shape OptimizationFEALightweighting

Overview

On a robotic arm, mass is expensive twice: the part itself weighs more, and every gram at the end of the arm demands more torque, bigger actuators, and more energy to move. This project took an existing arm component and cut its mass in half through shape optimization, guided at every step by stress analysis rather than guesswork.

Design approach

The optimization removed material from low-stress areas — the regions FEA showed were barely working — and trimmed stress concentrators, the sharp features where cracks start. The result keeps material only where the load path needs it, which also simplifies the geometry the part has to be manufactured from.

Validation

The final static stress analysis validated the design: maximum von Mises stress of 85.541 MPa with 0.321 mm peak displacement, and a minimum factor of safety of 5.88 — well above the 1.5 minimum requirement. With that much margin, the analysis also highlighted opportunities for further weight reduction or alternative material selection, while the optimized shape already improves manufacturability and cost-efficiency.

Design Optimization

Coat Hook

2024 · Comparative Generative Design Study

A comparative generative-design study across materials and manufacturing processes, run against a 4.0 factor-of-safety target — a small part used to answer a general question: which material and process actually win?

8.46 FOSDie-cast Aluminum AlSi10Mg — 0.2 mm max displacement
61–62 gPA 11 polymer iterations — lighter, but weaker
Generative DesignAdditive MfgDie Casting

Overview

A coat hook is a deliberately simple part, which makes it a clean test bed: one load case, one safety target, and no confounding geometry. The real subject of the study isn't the hook — it's the trade-off between polymer and metal, and between additive manufacturing and die casting, when a generative design tool is allowed to explore all four combinations.

Design approach

The study systematically compared PA 11 polymer with Aluminum AlSi10Mg, testing both additive manufacturing and die casting for each, and optimized every variant for weight and mechanical properties against the same 4.0 factor-of-safety target — so the differences in the results come from the material and process, not from moving goalposts.

Results

Die-cast Aluminum AlSi10Mg came out ahead with an 8.46 factor of safety — more than double the target — and just 0.2 mm of maximum displacement. The PA 11 polymer iterations were lighter at 61–62 g, but weaker. The conclusion: aluminum alloy paired with die casting provided the optimal mechanical performance, and the study quantifies exactly what the lighter polymer option would have cost in strength.

About Me

How I got here

The first moment I remember wanting to pursue mechanical engineering was working with my dad on his car as a kid. Doing basic maintenance and modding with him got me thinking about how parts and systems could be optimized — and it gave me a lasting passion for problem solving. I liked taking something apart, understanding why it worked the way it did, and figuring out how it could work better. Watching Iron Man fed the same spark — seeing someone build extraordinary machines in a garage made engineering feel less like a school subject and more like something I actually wanted to do.

That carried straight into hardware that has to survive real loads. With UCR Highlander Racing, I spent three years on the suspension team — sizing control arm tubing for buckling, verifying camber change per shim, modeling a full front corner for FEA and kinematics, and releasing drawings with GD&T so what we welded matched what we analyzed. That work taught me to design for manufacturing and validation at the same time, not as an afterthought.

At the Robotics & Explainable AI Lab (REAL), that hardware background met machine learning. I operated the ALOHA bimanual teleoperation system to generate demonstration datasets, built a custom camera mount to keep recordings consistent, and trained temporal action segmentation models — work that became a co-authored paper, Uncertainty-Aware Action Segmentation for Human-to-Robot Assembly Teleoperation.

What I do now

At Meta Superintelligence Labs, I lead a 30-operator robotics data team. My work sits between the physical setup and the data it produces: designing manipulation tasks, workspace layouts, and SOPs, holding trajectory quality to the standard autonomous policy training requires, troubleshooting hardware and software faults, interviewing and onboarding new hires to grow capacity, and training new team leads. Earlier improvements to cable routing, camera mounts, inventory tracking, and scheduling helped lift single-shift fleet efficiency from 22% to 50%.

My edge

Most mechanical engineers never train a model, and most ML teams never machine a part. I do both — and I lead the people who run the operation. That combination helps me tell whether a robot is failing on hardware, task design, or data, and then lead the fix. I brought the same approach to my senior capstone, leading a 5-person team building an EEG-controlled BCI wheelchair, with Gantt-chart planning and weekly technical presentations to faculty advisors.

Hands-on, not just CAD

I build what I design: CNC machining and manual lathe / mill work, in-house welding and soldering, and 3D printing in PLA and PETG. I am SolidWorks CSWA certified and also work in Fusion 360, FEA, and GD&T. I speak English and Mandarin, with conversational Cantonese and Vietnamese — useful when leading and training diverse operator teams.

How I work

Beyond the technical skills, what tends to matter most on a team is how someone works day to day. This is the approach I try to bring to every role:

  • Own the problem end to end — from diagnosing whether a failure is hardware, task design, or data, through to leading the fix and checking that it held.
  • Make the work repeatable — writing SOPs, workspace layouts, and quality standards so good results do not depend on one person being in the room.
  • Communicate clearly and often — weekly technical presentations and memo reports as a capstone lead, and regular onboarding and feedback while leading operators at Meta.
  • Grow the team’s capacity — interviewing and onboarding new hires, and training new team leads, not just completing my own tasks.
  • Stay hands-on under pressure — troubleshooting hardware and software faults on the floor, and improving the physical setup — cable routing, camera mounts, scheduling — when that is what is limiting the data.
